The blood count raises a suspicion. PATHEX names the test that settles it.

PATHEX reads an adult complete blood count, together with whatever other results and clinical context the clinician has, and returns a ranked suspicion, its confidence, the confirmatory test to order next, and the reasoning in plain language. It runs entirely on the clinician's own computer, needs no internet, and sends no patient data anywhere. It is rule-based, not machine learning: the same inputs always give the same answer, and every answer can be traced.

Where it stands · October 2026

An engineering prototype in clinical refinement — not yet validated

What this page shows is engineering work: a working knowledge base and reasoning system, tested end to end on synthetic cases. Clinical validation is the stage still ahead, and nothing here should be read as evidence of clinical accuracy.

What is built

  • The knowledge base for the adult blood count: 115 outcomes, 51 confirmatory tests and management suggestions on 80 of the outcomes.
  • The desktop application, which is the main product, with report upload and automatic reading of printed CBC reports.
  • Safety behaviour: refusals, safety blocks and data-quality checks (below).
  • End-to-end tests on synthetic cases through the real interface.

What comes next

  • Clinical refinement: a clinician reviews each threshold, route and wording against its cited source.
  • Validation against de-identified laboratory records where the confirmatory result is already known.
  • Only then any use in care, and only within the scope the validation supports.

How it reasons

A count suspects. Another result confirms the direction. The clinician orders the test.

A blood count alone rarely proves a cause, so PATHEX does not diagnose from it. It raises a suspicion and names the test that would confirm or refute it. Each additional result the clinician supplies — iron studies, a film comment, a calcium, the clinical context — can turn a vague picture into a specific, actionable one.

Values→classified against cited thresholds→combined into patterns→conflicts checked→one outcome→confidence band→confirmatory test→management suggestion

Discrimination index panel

For microcytic anaemia, PATHEX also shows four published indices that help separate iron deficiency from thalassaemia trait: Mentzer, RDW index, Patra and CRUISE. Each row states which way that index runs and where this patient falls, because two of the four lean towards trait when the value is low and two when it is high. The panel is labelled as triage only: iron studies and HbA₂ confirm, the indices never do.

Data sources

Thresholds cite named sources, not a laboratory's printed range

The knowledge base keeps a register of 144 sources, and its rules carry 207 citations into it. The engine checks those citations every time it starts, and refuses to start if one points to a source that is not registered.

  • Guidelines and public-health bodies: WHO, NICE, the British Society for Haematology, ICSH, KDIGO, the International Myeloma Working Group, ICMR, and India's Ministry of Health including Anemia Mukt Bharat.
  • Peer-reviewed literature, 75 of the sources with a PubMed identifier, and StatPearls for clinical background.
  • India-relevant knowledge: alignment with Anemia Mukt Bharat, Indian epidemiology, carrier prevalence, and altitude adjustment of haemoglobin.

What is special

A source that does not hold up is flagged, not hidden

  • Reference ranges printed on a lab report are never the standard. They vary by analyser and laboratory; only published sources define a threshold.
  • Every source carries a status. Where a cited source turned out not to support a value, the register records it; sources that were withdrawn or could not be located are marked as not citable.
  • Citation is not validation. A clinician still has to review each threshold against its source; that review is the clinical-refinement stage now under way.

Safety and privacy by design

Refuse rather than guess

Scope gates

Patients under 18 and pregnant patients are refused, with the reason stated, because their reference ranges differ. The refusal is enforced twice: in the application and again inside the knowledge base.

Safety blocks

Severe anaemia stops the subtype analysis and routes to urgent review. Physiologically impossible values, such as an MCHC of 39.7, return "cannot evaluate — repeat the count" instead of an answer.

No silent drops

A result in an unrecognised unit is refused with a message, not ignored. A missing value is never filled in, and a value the system does not understand never reads as normal.

Stays on the device

The application works offline and makes no external network calls. Name, date of birth, ABHA ID, record number and contact never leave the computer.

De-identified export

For quality review, records export without name or ID; age becomes a 10-year band and the date a month. Checked on a synthetic record: no name, ID or exact age in the export.

Never a dose

Management suggestions are directional. No dose is ever produced, and every suggestion points to the local formulary and the confirmatory test first.

Architecture

A blind engine and a separate knowledge base

PATHEX is a knowledge base running on AMEKU, a general rule engine that contains no clinical vocabulary. Every threshold, outcome and wording lives in the knowledge base as data, and the engine only executes it. That separation is what makes the reasoning traceable: each output can be followed back to the rule, threshold and cited source that produced it.

Local adaptation

A new region is a data change, not an engine change

Region-specific knowledge — reference ranges, national programme alignment, local epidemiology, altitude — sits in its own data files. From an engineering point of view, adapting PATHEX to another country means adding or replacing those files; the engine does not change. Clinically, every new region still needs its own source review and validation before use.

Future scope

Validate first, then widen the scope

Each step must be validated on its own before the next one is used clinically.

  1. Adult CBC knowledge base
    • 115 outcomes, desktop application, safety behaviour
    • Built; tested end to end on synthetic cases
  2. Clinical refinement and validation
    • Clinician review of thresholds, routes and wording
    • Validation on de-identified records with known confirmatory results
    • Current stage
  3. Pregnancy wing
    • Pregnancy-specific haemoglobin thresholds and reasoning
    • Pregnant patients are refused today
  4. Neonatal wing
    • Age-specific reference ranges for newborns
    • Children are refused today
  5. Blood-cell level
    • Reading cell morphology on the peripheral smear, so the most decisive extra input is produced, not only typed in
  6. Timeline
    • Reasoning over a patient's series of counts: direction, speed, chronicity and response to treatment
    • Designed; not yet built

The timeline layer, as designed

Today PATHEX answers "is this count abnormal?". The timeline layer would answer "in which direction, how fast, and for how long has this patient's blood been changing?" — deterministically, on the same engine. Its design proceeds in stages, each with an exit test: a series contract, then velocity and trend, chronicity, confounders such as a recent transfusion, and treatment response, then presentation. A trend is never asserted from too few counts or across a confounding event. This is a design, not built software, and no timeline durations are promised.

After validation · who it helps

Anaemia is common; the specialist who reads the count often is not

1.92 billion

people had anaemia in 2021, 24.3% of the world's population.1

244 million

women with anaemia in WHO's South-East Asia region, and 106 million in Africa — the two regions carrying the heaviest burden.2

57% · 25%

of women and men aged 15–49 in India were anaemic in 2019–21.3

Who benefits, and how

  • Doctors at primary health centres, who interpret blood counts without a haematologist nearby: a structured reading and the specific next test, in seconds and offline.
  • Patients: fewer missed follow-ups — the ferritin that was never ordered, the film that was never requested — and fewer avoidable harms, such as iron given to a thalassaemia carrier, or iron deficiency in an adult accepted without asking where the iron is being lost.
  • Laboratories: implausible values and likely reporting errors are flagged rather than passed through.
  • Public-health programmes, such as anaemia programmes, that need consistent, de-identified records for quality review.
  • Other low- and middle-income countries, where the burden is highest, through local data files and local validation.

Free where it matters most

After successful validation, the licensed version of PATHEX will be provided free of cost to government primary health centres and to charitable hospitals and clinics.

Read these numbers carefully

They show the size of the need, not a number of people PATHEX will help. PATHEX does not treat anaemia; it helps a clinician interpret a blood count and choose the next test. Its current scope is non-pregnant adults, so the largest groups above — young children and pregnant women — are exactly the ones the next wings are for. It will help no one until it is validated.
